Does social media help the post-protest phase, to transition to democracy? Case study on Egypt. 

- Theory: social media has different effects in the protest process (lowers costs of mobilisation and creates unity and numbers) compared to the transition process, when stabilisation is needed (increases polarisation, fear and fragmentation, and it leads to a failure of democratic transition). 

- Clustering: during protest times, social media can help build coalition across groups with a shared goal. But as time goes on, people increasingly segregate into ideological communities or “clusters". Over time, these separate bubbles diverge sharply in perceptions and narratives. 

- Fear amplification: in transitions, institutions aren’t stable, so people feel more uncertain about their safety, legitimacy, and what is permissible. Social media accelerates dramatic, emotional content, which travels rapidly within clusters and can heighten fear, mistrust, and social anxiety. This heightening of fear can discourage moderate voices, push people to more extreme positions, or feed cycles of crisis escalation.

- Role of the State: the Egyptian state and military learned from 2011 that ignoring social media was dangerous. By 2012-2013, they actively used it for surveillance (monitoring activists), flooding and counter-mobilisation.

Important: METHODOLOGY:

- Data:
1) Twitter:** every public tweet containing "Egypt" (English or Arabic), Jan 2011 – Aug 2013. 62M tweets, >7M unique users, bought from Topsy (a data vendor that no longer exists). Analysis focuses on 1 Jul 2012 – 30 Jun 2013 (Morsi's election to the protest before the coup): ~25M tweets

2) Facebook: 7 public pages; 1,000 posts randomly sampled per page over the same year; all comments extracted (593,428)

- Why a keyword-based sample (not hashtags or seed accounts)
1) Hashtag samples can be biased because some hashtags are tied to one political camp.
2) Seed-account samples(built outward from high-profile users) can leave out low-profile, non-mainstream voices.

- Method
1) Network clustering: retweet networks split into communities every two weeks with the Louvain method, giving 26 snapshots. The 10 largest clusters per snapshot were kept.

2) Superclusters: two or more clusters sharing >20% of their retweets.

3) Tracking clusters over time: the Jaccard coefficient (membership overlap: 0 = no shared members, 1 = identical) was computed between clusters in consecutive fortnights.
   - Best match ≥ 0.1 → same cluster continues.
   - Best match < 0.1 → cluster "dissolution".

4) Qualitative labelling:
   - Clusters identified from anchor accounts (TV stations, newspapers, prominent figures, network-central users).
   - The 15 most-retweeted tweets per cluster were read by hand.
   - This catches errors that pure network analysis would make.
   - It also screens out irrelevant clusters (e.g. Google Earth images, FIFA football).

- 1981: imposition of martial law 
- 1989: beginning of democratic transition 

Progress: how to assess it 
- 1989: after 40 years of communist rule, roundtable talks between communist leadership and opposition parties. 4th June 1989, partly free elections.  
- Transition towards a free-market economy, poland became the first country of central-eastern europe to join the third wave of democratisation (happened across 1974-1990)

Assessing democracy in poland following 4 main procedural dimensions: 
- Representation: the representatives must resemble in some way those who they represent Acting in the interests of the represented
- Participation: citizens should have equal opportunity to participate in democratic agenda
- Competition: vital because it provides selection for the democratic elites. 
- Accountability: guarantees that political elites respond for their actions

First 2 decades of democracy - Poland:
Improved quality from a procedural perspective
- Representation became more inclusive, from fragmentation and volatility to quasi institutionalised system, tribunals and government accountability functioned, 1997 Constitution established a new framework of governance and to comply with EU -> access to EU in 2004
- Most notable democratic deficit: participation. Electoral turnout remained pretty low, and non-electoral participation also remained significantly low (low civic engagement) -> polish citizens were passive democrats. 
- Economic progress: Poland’s democratisation -> economic transformation. GDP per capita went from 1600$ in 1989 to 31000$ in 2026. 

From 2015: Democratic Backsliding
- Part of the Third wave of Democratisation (105 authocratisation episodes across 75 countries)
- Victor Orban initiated an illiberal turn and followed by Poland when PiS went to power, after the 2015 parliamentary elections. 
- Weakening of checks and balances, expansion of executive power, undermining of judicial independence and censorship of media and civil society -> democratic erosion promised as reform 

Feminist perspective on backsliding 
- Backsliding is often accompanied by growing opposition to gender equality and feminism
- Right wing populism goes hand in hand with antifeminism: backsliding disproportionately affects women and weaker groups 
- State feminism was instituted after 2015 elections: anti-feminist and hetero-normative discourses used by PiS. It framed feminist and lgbt groups as foreign interference to polish values. Heavily influenced by the Catholic Church, it started a strong anti-reproductive rights tasks, endorsing the constitutional tribunals in 2020 to ban the right to abortion. It cut funding to feminism and civil rights based organisations. 
- Hungary, US, South Korea and Brazil also saw an anti-feministic wave that accompanied democratic backsliding (state antifeminism amplification in South Korea in 2020)

How feminist resistance is linked to democratic resilience
- Democratic resilience: capacity to resist democratic backsliding. Since backsliding is put in place by the ruling party, resilience is carried out by alternation in power. Specifically, by removal of an incumbent leader, by election of an opposition party or by impeachment 
- Feminist perspective on resilience: how both non-electoral and electoral participation can enhance democratic resilience by mobilising resilience, especially with a focus on women. This is especially visible when civil and women rights come under threat. 
- Poland: saw a major wave of feminist protests after 2015, with participation reaching 400.000 people following the constitutional tribunal ruling the ban on abortion in 2020. Women were nearly twice as many than men, young women dominated with 43% aged 18-24.

Women’s electoral participation: 
- Parliamentary elections of Oct 2023 saw an extraordinary turnout of 74% in Poland, with women’s turnout exceeding that of men. 
- Voting preferences: 56% of women supported the democratic coalition (Civic coalition of the left + the third way). 
- The gender gap in voting was more pronounced among young voters
- Moreover, support for the far-right confederation was significantly lower in women than men
- These patterns of gender differences in voting (women -> more democratic and liberal parties) appear also in other countries. 

Poland: Democratic Recovery 
- Happened after 2023, it still remains below its 2015 peak. 
- It confirms that redemocratisation is long and hard: restoring liberal democracies presents numerous challenges. 1) re-establishing rule of law, 2) re-building democratic institutions, 3) restoring checks and balances, 4) protecting civil liberties, 5) protecting media freedom 
- Redemocratisation should not be understood as a process of re-establishing pre-backsliding institutions. It offers an opportunity to build a more inclusive democracy. 

Ahead of the 2027 parliamentary election: 
- Recent poll shows significant combined support of the far right: Confederation, freedom and independence reaches 14%, while Confederation of the Polish Crown (new party) reaches 11%. Combined 25% of the vote, with PiS at 21%. 
- This is a significant increase compared to the 2023 elections, where the CFI only had 7% of votes.
